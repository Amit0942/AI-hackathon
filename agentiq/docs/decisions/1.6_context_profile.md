## 2. POI types, footfall, and the `scale` field

`est_daily_footfall` ranges from a median of 1,491 (`hospital`) to 11,003
(`stadium_arena`), with `museum` (4,083) and `hotel_convention` (3,240)
also carrying outsized pull relative to their count.

The ordinal `scale` field (`neighborhood` / `minor` / `major` / `flagship`)
does **not** track footfall monotonically:

| scale | median footfall |
| --- | --- |
| minor | 440 |
| neighborhood | 1,732 |
| major | 5,442 |
| flagship | 14,038 |

`minor` sits *below* `neighborhood`, not above it. **`scale` cannot be used
as an ordinal multiplier on footfall** — it is a distinct, unordered
categorical judgement (likely encoding something like "how established" a
POI is, independent of its raw pull) and must be modelled as its own term,
with `est_daily_footfall` doing the magnitude work.

## 3. The POI-proximity radius

Coverage of the network's 910 locations rises with radius, but with sharply
diminishing marginal signal:

| radius | POIs captured | locations covered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.2 km | 631 | 49.0% |
| 0.3 km | 894 | 65.4% |
| 0.5 km | 1,201 | 86.4% |
| 0.75 km | 1,322 | 95.5% |
| 1.0 km | 1,367 | 99.3% |

Footfall scale rises sharply with distance bucket (median 815 at 0-100m vs
11,750 at 1-1.5km) — but this is a **selection effect, not a visibility
signal**: large anchors (malls, stadiums) get logged as the nearest POI to a
location even a kilometre out, while small neighbourhood POIs are only
recorded when genuinely close by. **0.3–0.5 km is the radius that captures
real, local signal**; most of the coverage gain beyond 0.5 km adds duplicate
coverage to already-covered locations rather than new signal, and risks
attributing a flagship POI's pull to a screen a real pedestrian would not
associate with it.

`side_of_road` is **not** a proxy for distance — near-side (median 0.219km)
and far-side (median 0.234km) POIs sit at nearly identical distances. It
must be kept as an independent visibility term in the D1 model, not folded
into the distance-decay function.

## 6. Ridership — scheduled vs actual, and the daypart curve

The schedule is directionally wrong in opposite ways for weekday vs
weekend: actual ridership runs **+7.6%** above `estimated_ridership` on
weekdays but **−13.3%** below it on weekends. A single blanket correction
factor would be wrong for one or the other. **Rule: always use
`ridership_actuals`, never `estimated_ridership`, for anything
demand-facing** — the schedule is a planning artifact, not a measurement.

Holidays behave like a heavier weekend (median 83 riders vs weekday's 173
and weekend's 52) — confirming they don't belong in the weekday baseline,
but they are not simply interchangeable with weekends either.

**No scheduled trip starts before 05:00 or after 23:00** — time block 1
(00:00–04:00) has **zero ridership rows**, confirmed as a genuine absence of
overnight transit service, not a join defect. Any exposure model for block 1
must fall back to a non-ridership signal (POI-only or a stated flat
assumption).

### The normalised daypart curve (the exit-criterion deliverable)

| time_block_id | label | weekday share | weekend share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 00:00-04:00 | — (no service) | — (no service) |
| 2 | 04:00-08:00 | 20.9% | 9.1% |
| 3 | 08:00-12:00 | 27.0% | 24.1% |
| 4 | 12:00-16:00 | 12.1% | 31.4% |
| 5 | 16:00-20:00 | 30.1% | 25.7% |
| 6 | 20:00-24:00 | 9.8% | 9.8% |

Weekday ridership peaks on the two commute blocks (3 and 5). **Weekend
ridership shifts to a genuinely different peak block (4, not 3 or 5)** —
this is a reshaped curve, not a scaled-down weekday one. A single curve
reused for both day types would misplace the weekend peak by a full block,
directly undermining any brief that asks for weekend weighting.
