## 3. Occupancy — the booking-expansion transform

**Method.** A sweep-line expansion (`occupancy_timeline()`): each booking
contributes a `+slots_booked_per_day` event on `start_date` and a matching
`-slots_booked_per_day` event on `end_date + 1`, grouped by
`(screen_id, time_block_id)` and cumulatively summed by date. Validated
against a brute-force per-row overlap check on synthetic data before running
on the real tables (see the notebook).

| measure | value |
| --- | --- |
| Non-zero (screen, block, date) rows, full history | 276,866 |
| Peak occupied slots observed anywhere | **6** |

The peak matches the Step 1.4 capacity ceiling exactly — this booking-level
expansion and Step 1.4's separate sweep-line proof agree independently, which
is further confirmation the 6-slot ceiling is a real constraint, not a
sampling artifact.

On the as-of date itself (2026-08-19), 662 screen×block pairs carry live
occupancy, with a median of 4 and a 75th percentile already at the 6-slot
ceiling — i.e. a material share of currently-active bookings are already at
full exclusivity on that specific day.

**Carry-forward:** this transform is the one Step 6.1's scarcity signal and
Phase 7's availability constraint must both reuse — it is now proven correct
independently of the Step 1.4 sweep, not just internally consistent.

## 4. Bundles — the de-duplication trap is real and larger than it looks

| is_bundle | lines | deals | lines/deal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| False | 55,485 | 55,485 | 1.0 |
| True | 135,624 | 1,277 | 106.2 |

Summing `deal_total_value` at the line level (as a naive `groupby` would)
gives **$805,767,157,205**. Summing it correctly — once per deal, after
`drop_duplicates("deal_id")` — gives **$2,447,304,569**: a **329x**
overstatement, not the ~106x a naive read of "lines per deal" would suggest.

The gap between 106x and 329x is itself informative: it means bundle value is
**not evenly spread across bundles**. The naive sum is mathematically
`Σ(deal_total_value × lines_in_that_deal)`, so it's dominated by whichever
bundles have both a high value *and* a high line count — verified by
reconstructing the naive total exactly from per-deal `(value × line_count)`
products. **Any revenue rollup must group and de-duplicate by `deal_id`
first; summing `deal_total_value` (or line-level values without a bundle
adjustment) over raw booking rows is silently wrong by more than two orders
of magnitude.**

### 4.1 Bundle vs. standalone pricing

| is_bundle | lines (settled) | median $/slot/day | mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| False | 30,525 | 79.86 | 88.36 |
| True | 81,202 | 72.26 | 77.73 |

Bundled lines price roughly 9.5% below standalone lines at the median. This
is evidence for treating a bundle as a portfolio-priced deal (Phase 7.3) — the
per-line price is not simply the standalone rate, and bundle-level pricing
must be modelled as its own artifact rather than derived by averaging line
prices.

## 5. Slot-count pricing: the discount is real but not uniform across screen types

### 5.1 Network-level pattern (settled bookings only)

| slots_booked_per_day | lines | median $/slot/day | vs. 1 slot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 36,690 | 76.70 | 0.0% |
| 2 | 25,024 | 73.73 | −3.9% |
| 3 | 16,199 | 72.46 | −5.5% |
| 4 | 14,069 | 72.58 | −5.4% |
| 5 | 9,367 | 72.41 | −5.6% |
| 6 | 10,378 | 71.47 | −6.8% |

This reproduces the Step 1.4 §4.2 pattern on the settled-only subset: price
per slot falls, monotonically apart from a flat 3→4 step, as more slots are
bought — up to a 6.8% discount at full exclusivity (6 slots) vs. a single
slot.

### 5.2 Confound check — does the discount survive within `screen_type × time_block_id`?

Fitting a slope of median price-per-slot vs. `slots_booked_per_day`
**within each of the 24 `screen_type × time_block_id` cells** (6 screen-types
× ... actually 4 screen types × 6 blocks = 24 cells, each with all 6 slot
counts present):

| result | value |
| --- | --- |
| Cells with a fittable slope | 24 / 24 |
| Cells with a negative slope (discount holds) | 13 / 24 (54.2%) |
| Slope range | −1.56 to +0.87 |

**This is the single most important finding in this step: the network-level
discount is not a uniform effect — it is concentrated in specific screen
types and inverts in others.**

| screen_type | typical slope sign | pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `bus` | negative in all 6 blocks (−0.72 to −1.56) | strongest, most consistent discount |
| `metro_station` | negative in 4 of 6 blocks | discount holds on peak/midday blocks, weakens at night |
| `metro_rail_coach` | mixed, small magnitude (−0.16 to +0.43) | little real slot-count effect either way |
| `bus_stop` | **positive** in 5 of 6 blocks (+0.10 to +0.87) | price per slot *rises* with more slots booked |

**Consequence for Phase 6:** the raw §5.1 table is confounded by mix — it
partly reflects that `bus` and `metro_station` inventory (which show a real
discount) dominate the settled-booking counts. `bus_stop`'s inverted
relationship would be invisible in a single network-wide fit. The base-rate
model must include `screen_type` (and ideally `screen_type × slot_count`
interaction terms), not fit a single slot-count discount curve across the
whole network. This also means Phase 3's attention/impressions curve cannot
assume the same concavity parameters for every screen type — `bus_stop`
screens in particular may not exhibit diminishing per-slot value the way
`bus` and `metro_station` screens clearly do.

## 6. Lost leads — calibrating the price cap

Of 1,450 lost leads, 531 (36.6%) never reached a quote (`initial_inquiry`)
— `quoted_price_per_slot_per_day` and `client_target_price_per_slot_per_day`
are correctly null there, not missing data.

### 6.1 By sales stage reached

| stage | leads | had a quote | median price gap | median negotiation rounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| initial_inquiry | 531 | 0 | — | 0 |
| quote_sent | 415 | 415 | 28.4% | 0 |
| negotiating | 281 | 281 | 7.2% | 2 |
| verbal_agreement | 141 | 141 | 4.4% | 3 |
| contract_sent | 82 | 82 | 2.8% | 4 |

The gap shrinks monotonically the further a deal progresses — mechanically
expected (later stages are the ones close enough to negotiate), but it
confirms the data behaves consistently and the gap is a real proxy for deal
health, not noise.

### 6.2 Price-gap buckets vs. how close the deal got

| price gap | leads | % reaching verbal-agreement-or-later | median rounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≤0% (we quoted at or below target) | 125 | 38.4% | 2 |
| 0–5% | 113 | 40.7% | 2 |
| 5–10% | 104 | 44.2% | 2 |
| 10–15% | 25 | 48.0% | 3 |
| 15–20% | 52 | 7.7% | 0 |
| \>20% | 341 | 3.5% | 0 |

**This is the empirical willingness-to-pay ceiling Phase 6.3 needs.** Deals
survive to verbal-agreement-or-later at roughly 40–48% of the time up through
a 15% price gap — then the survival rate collapses to single digits above
15%. **A price cap calibrated around a 10–15% gap over client target is
defensible; above ~15–20% the deal is essentially dead regardless of
negotiation effort** (note the 15–20% and >20% buckets also show 0 median
negotiation rounds — those deals mostly didn't get contested at all, they
just died).

Price/budget-related reasons dominate the top of the list, reinforcing that
the price-gap signal in §6.2 is the dominant, not incidental, driver of loss.

### 6.4 Lead-to-loss cycle length by stage (recency-decay input)

| stage reached | median days lead → loss |
| --- | --- |
| initial_inquiry | 4.0 |
| quote_sent | 8.0 |
| negotiating | 19.0 |
| verbal_agreement | 28.0 |
| contract_sent | 39.5 |

Cycle length scales with stage reached, as expected. This gives Phase 6's
recency-decay function a concrete scale: a lead sitting at `negotiating` for
longer than ~19 days is already past the typical resolution window for that
stage and should be treated as ageing, not fresh pipeline pressure.

### 6.5 Competitor pressure

| competitor_mentioned | leads | median price gap | median rounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| False | 1,193 | 22.9% | 0 |
| True | 257 | 3.4% | 1 |

Leads where a competitor was explicitly named lost at a **much smaller**
price gap (3.4% vs 22.9%) — i.e. competitive deals are lost on razor-thin
margins, not blown budgets. This is a distinct signal from the general
price-gap curve in §6.2 and should be modelled separately: competitive
pressure lowers the effective price cap, it doesn't just correlate with a
larger gap.
